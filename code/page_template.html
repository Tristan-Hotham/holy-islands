Holy islands

Monks, hermits and the edge of the world. Of {{coverage.n|int}} monasteries, abbeys, priories and hermitages in Europe that Wikidata can place on a map, {{islands.n|int}} stand on islands. Which ones, from when, and how far out to sea?

Dr Tristan Hotham · Independent researcher · 2026 · Data: Wikidata (CC0), via QLever; GSHHG shorelines; Natural Earth
{{coverage.n|int}}monastic houses in Europe with a location in Wikidata

In the sixth and seventh centuries monks from Ireland and Britain went looking for a desert in the ocean. They found it on Iona and Lindisfarne, on Bardsey and Caldey, and on Skellig Michael, a rock twelve kilometres off Kerry where they built stone beehive cells on ledges above the Atlantic. Further south the same idea had already taken root on the Lérins islands off Cannes, and later it would carry hermits to the Faroes and Iceland. An island was a cloister made by God: cut off, hard to reach, close to the edge of the known world.

How unusual was that, really? Wikidata, the open database behind Wikipedia, records {{coverage.n|int}} monasteries, abbeys, priories, convents, friaries and hermitages in Europe with coordinates. I checked every one against a detailed world shoreline to see whether it stands on an island, how big the island is and how far it lies from the mainland, and lined that up against when each house was founded and when it was dissolved. Slide through the centuries, keep only the islands, search for a place or a house, and click any dot.

    02 · Through timeThe early houses went to sea

    Only {{coverage.dated_n|int}} houses ({{coverage.dated_pct|pc}}) have a foundation date in Wikidata, so the time comparison rests on a third of the map. Within that third the pattern is clear. Of the {{compare.all_pre800.n|int}} houses dated before 800, {{compare.all_pre800.isl|int}} ({{compare.all_pre800.pct|f1}}%) are on islands: Lérins and Cabrera in the Mediterranean, Bardsey, Caldey, Iona, Douglas on Man and Mont-Saint-Michel on the Atlantic side, and Reichenau, the two Chiemsee islands and the Île Barbe in lakes and rivers. From 1100 to 1299, the great age of Cistercians, canons and friars, the share falls to {{compare.all_p3.pct|f1}}% of {{compare.all_p3.n|int}} houses, and it stays near that level ({{compare.all_p4.pct|f1}}%) afterwards.

    The Atlantic edge is where the habit was strongest. Of the {{compare.early_atl.n|int}} dated houses founded there before 1100, {{compare.early_atl.isl|int}} are on islands ({{compare.early_atl.pct|pc}}, though with so few the true figure could lie anywhere from {{compare.early_atl.lo|pc}} to {{compare.early_atl.hi|pc}}). Of the {{compare.late_cont.n|int}} houses founded in France and Central Europe from 1100 on, just {{compare.late_cont.isl|int}} are ({{compare.late_cont.pct|f1}}%). So yes: the early Atlantic foundations chose islands far more often than the later Continental ones. The caveat is the size of the early sample, and the next section shows why it is so small.

    03 · WhereScotland, Wales and the Aegean

    Counting every house, dated or not, the island share is highest in Wales ({{regions.2.pct|f1}}%) and Scotland ({{regions.1.pct|f1}}%), then the Mediterranean ({{regions.11.pct|f1}}%) and Ireland ({{regions.0.pct|f1}}%). The Mediterranean has by far the most island houses, {{regions.11.isl|int}}, {{compare.med_grc_tur_pct|pc}} of them on the Greek and Turkish islands of the Aegean and the Sea of Marmara, most of them Orthodox monasteries. France and Central Europe, which hold over half the houses in the data, have almost none ({{compare.cont_all.pct|f1}}%), and {{compare.cont_lake|int}} of their {{compare.cont_all.isl|int}} island houses are in lakes and rivers, such as Reichenau on Lake Constance and the two islands of the Chiemsee.

    04 · How farThe furthest from the mainland

    Measured to the nearest point of continental Europe, Africa, Great Britain or Ireland, half of the sea-island houses are within {{far.median_sea|int}} km of it. The furthest are the convents of the Azores, founded after Portugal settled the islands in the 1400s: the Convent of Carmo at Horta is {{far.top.0.d|int}} km from the mainland. Madeira, the Canaries and Malta follow. On the big islands that this study counts as mainland, Iceland's monasteries are further still: Þingeyrar, founded in {{far.big_top.0.y|raw}}, is {{far.big_top.0.d|int}} km from the nearest mainland coast.

    Island houses furthest from the mainland

    One house per island; with Skellig Michael and Iona for scale. Click a bar to find the house.

    The Celtic holy islands are, by comparison, close in: Skellig Michael is {{far.skellig.d|f1}} km off Kerry and Iona {{far.iona.d|int}} km from the Scottish mainland, though both feel much further in a small boat. In Britain and Ireland the furthest out is Cille Bharra on Barra in the Outer Hebrides, {{far.atl.0.d|int}} km from the mainland. Iceland's papar, the Irish hermits said to have been there before the Norse, and the early hermits of the Faroes do not appear in Wikidata as monasteries at all: Iceland and the Faroes have just {{coverage.natl_n|int}} houses between them.

    05 · The endDissolved, abandoned, and when

    {{dissolution.n|int}} houses ({{dissolution.pct|pc}}) have a date when they were dissolved or abandoned. The dates come in waves. {{dissolution.waves.dissolution_ew|int}} fall in England and Wales between 1536 and 1541, Henry VIII's Dissolution of the Monasteries: {{dissolution.ew_pct|pc}} of all the dated closures there. {{dissolution.waves.france_rev|int}} French houses closed in the Revolution (1789 to 1795) and {{dissolution.waves.german_1802_1811|int}} in the German lands in the secularisations of 1802 to 1811. The Reformation century (1520 to 1599) and the revolutionary era (1780 to 1815) between them hold {{dissolution.reform_rev_pct|pc}} of every dated closure.

    When houses were dissolved or abandoned

    Houses with a Wikidata dissolution date, by decade. Hover for counts.

    The Viking age, which fell first and hardest on the island monasteries (Lindisfarne in 793, Iona from 795), barely registers: {{dissolution.waves.viking_all|int}} closures dated between 793 and 1066, none on an island. That is a gap in the record, not in history. Wikidata's entries mostly describe the houses that were later refounded and later dissolved: Lindisfarne Priory is the Benedictine cell of {{lindis.y|raw}}, and Bardsey Abbey's dissolution date is {{lindis.bardsey_d|raw}}. Island houses are less often recorded as closed: {{dissolution.isl_pct|f1}}% have a date, against {{dissolution.main_pct|f1}}% on the mainland. Many are Greek and Turkish island monasteries with no recorded closure, but even leaving those out the figure is only {{dissolution.isl_nongt_pct|f1}}%.

    07 · The gapsA map of who keeps records

    This is a map of Wikidata, not of medieval monasticism. Germany alone has {{coverage.deu_pct|pc}} of the houses. Ireland, with thousands of early church sites in its national monuments record, has {{coverage.irl_n|int}} here, and Skellig Michael itself has no foundation date. Coverage per head ranges from {{coverage.per_m_top.0.per_m|int}} houses per million people in {{coverage.per_m_top.0.name|raw}} to under {{coverage.per_m_low.4.per_m|int}} in Norway, Ukraine, Hungary, Turkey and Finland. Dating is thin too: {{coverage.dated_pct|pc}} of houses have a foundation date, and {{coverage.rc_pct|pc}} of those dates are only to the century.

    08 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Houses. Every Wikidata item that is an instance of a subclass of monastery, abbey, priory, hermitage or clochán and has coordinates, retrieved through QLever in {{meta.retrieved|raw}}: {{meta.n_items_world|int}} items worldwide. I kept those in Europe (with Iceland, the Faroes, the Azores, Madeira, the Canaries, Cyprus and Turkey) and dropped {{meta.excluded_class|int}} items that are only wayside shrines, lodgings, town houses of abbeys or Buddhist monasteries, leaving {{coverage.n|int}}. Region comes from the country and the Natural Earth province; the Atlantic Celtic edge is Ireland (north and south), Scotland, Wales, historic Brittany and Galicia.

    Islands. Each house was placed on the GSHHG full-resolution shoreline, which includes islets as small as Skellig Michael. A point in the sea within {{meta.snap_km|f1}} km of land goes to that land. An island is land under 1,000 km²; Great Britain, Ireland and every bigger island are treated as mainland, as their houses were not choosing an island in any meaningful sense (counting them as islands lifts the share to {{islands.any_pct|f1}}%). Islands under 25 km², the classic holy island, hold {{islands.small_n|int}} houses. The shoreline misses many small lake and river islands, so I checked by hand every house whose name says island (Inis, Inch, Ynys, Isle, Île, Insel and so on) and moved {{islands.n_override|int}} onto islands; inland islands are still probably undercounted. Distances are to the nearest continental Europe, Africa, Great Britain or Ireland coast, or for lake islands to the lake shore.

    Dates. Inception is the date a house was founded, which for a refounded house may be its last foundation rather than its first. Wikidata stores a century as a full date (the 12th century as 1101 or 1100), so years ending in 01 or 00 on 1 January are read as the middle of that century. Reading a 00 year as the century it begins instead (1100 as the 12th century) leaves the early Atlantic share at {{sens.atl_pre1100.pct|f1}}% and moves the all-Europe shares by a few tenths of a point ({{sens.p1.pct|f1}}% before 800, {{sens.p3.pct|f1}}% for 1100 to 1299). Intervals are Wilson 95% intervals.

    What this is not. Not a census of monasteries: coverage depends on which national lists have been loaded into Wikidata, early sites are often filed as churches or archaeological sites rather than monasteries, and labels are mostly English. Counts compare records, not religious history.
